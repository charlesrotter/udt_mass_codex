# G73 lay report

The kaleidoscope idea survives, but we can now say exactly what it needs.

An ordinary smooth lens can radically change the appearance of one particular picture while still
being reversible. If we know the lens perfectly, we can reconstruct the original. That is the
current regular UDT response: the unique cosmic mass pattern may remain decisive even though UDT
distorts what we see.

A stronger kaleidoscope does something more. It copies the same source feature into several places,
folds directions together, or squashes almost every orientation onto a preferred geometric axis.
Those operations can impose repeated motifs and symmetries that overwhelm the source's original
organization.

The existing G68 controls do not do that strongly. Their largest directional stretching difference
is under half a percent. But those controls cover only a small regular stationary tile, not the
complete sky near extreme dilation. They therefore neither prove nor disprove the cosmic
kaleidoscope.

The next object to map is not another collection of random source images. It is the optical layout
itself: where the complete UDT sky relation is one-to-one, where it repeats images, where several
paths arrive, and where it folds or becomes singular. Once that layout is known, broad source
ensembles can test whether geometry or the unique mass distribution controls each observed feature.
