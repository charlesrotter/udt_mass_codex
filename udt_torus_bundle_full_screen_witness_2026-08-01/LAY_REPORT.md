# Lay report

The previous audit found assembly instructions. This time we actually built the building.

Picture a three-dimensional corridor whose cross-section is a flexible two-dimensional screen.
After walking once around the corridor, the far end is glued back to the beginning. The gluing may
return the screen unchanged, flipped, rotated, sheared, or stretched in one direction while being
compressed in another. This is the registered bundle/lattice description, not an assertion that
bare topology alone fixes a screen.

We constructed a smooth screen shape all the way around each of the eight registered gluing types.
The screen never tears, collapses, or changes signature. Every derivative matches at the final
seam, so the join is genuinely smooth rather than patched with a crease. Because the resulting
space is compact, its spatial geometry is complete. Adding the constant reciprocal clock direction
also gives a complete four-dimensional off-shell metric.

There were two especially interesting surprises.

First, the eight tested global assembly representatives become only seven different local metric
matching rules. Returning every
screen vector with a minus sign is globally different from returning it unchanged, but distances
and angles are quadratic: two minus signs cancel. Local metric measurements therefore cannot tell
those two global constructions apart by themselves.

Second, six assembly rules allow the screen to keep one constant shape around the entire loop. The
parabolic and hyperbolic rules do not. They force the screen to change shape somewhere along the
loop, no matter which positive initial shape is chosen. That is a direct example of registered
global bundle monodromy forcing local geometric variation within the chosen block/lattice
presentation.

This still does not tell us which loop is the universe, whether any branch contains matter, or how
the universe chooses a branch. It gives us a real second completed geometry in which the proposed
global-to-local relationship can be studied honestly.
