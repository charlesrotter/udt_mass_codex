# Lay report — what the transport atlas found

Think of the three defect circles as seams in a woven three-dimensional cell. If you walk a tiny
circle around one seam, an arrow representing the metric-derived direction turns all the way around.
That looks dramatic locally.

But when the direction is treated correctly as a line—with no preferred arrowhead—the whole line
can be oriented consistently throughout the rest of the cell. In other words, the seams do **not**
force the line to come back reversed. The apparent winding is local bending, not a hidden binary
charge. This removes one tempting shortcut to particles or Hopf topology.

There is, however, a second and more interesting object. Pair the metric's clock direction with
that moving line. Together they form a little two-dimensional clock/ruler plane at every allowed
point. As you move through the angular geometry, that plane is boosted or tilted by a connection
that the metric itself determines. Changing the angular-screen shape changes this connection, and
changing the clock twist scales it exactly.

In the clock-and-line frame picked out by the metric itself, this connection is nonzero everywhere
away from the seams. Other mathematical frame choices can rewrite the connection; the curvature is
the frame-independent part used for the branch comparison.

So yes, this adds another piece to a possible background or substrate **geometry**: the cell has a
specific network of seams, local turning, and clock-to-angular transport. But it is not yet a
physical substrate model. We still do not have an equation saying which geometry nature chooses,
how it evolves, how density tunes it, or how matter emerges from it.

The clean gain is that we now know where not to look and where genuine structure remains:

- not in a topological flip of the defect line—it is globally untwisted;
- possibly in the branch-dependent transport of the combined clock/line plane;
- still open in global finite-loop behavior, dynamics, selection, density, and matter.
