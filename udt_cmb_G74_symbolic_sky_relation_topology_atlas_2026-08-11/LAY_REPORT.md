# Lay report — what the complete-sky topology test found

We finally turned the camera all the way around instead of looking through one narrow outward ray.

Three of the old smooth, non-mixing test geometries behave like ordinary clear lenses over the
whole sky: every viewing direction reaches one place on the surrounding sphere, and the mapping is
globally one-to-one.

Six smooth mixing-on geometries also behaved like ordinary distorted lenses at every sampled
direction. They bent and rearranged the sky, but we found no fold, duplicate sheet, or blind spot.
That is a strong numerical observation, not proof that a tiny unsampled fold cannot exist.

The surprise is that twelve other old profiles cannot honestly be used for this whole-sky test.
They were perfectly serviceable for the original outward ray, but their formulas are not smooth
enough at the center for inward-looking rays and curvature calculations. We did not “fix” them,
because any fix would invent new geometry.

So the current controls do not yet exhibit the kaleidoscope. They also do not rule it out. They tell
us exactly what is missing next: a genuinely global, center-regular complete metric family. Once we
have that, we can ask whether it folds or repeats the sky. Only afterward should we shine realistic
matter patterns through it—including both visible-survey structure and a broad unresolved-matter
bracket.
