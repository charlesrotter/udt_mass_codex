# Lay report — what overlapping tapes actually add

Imagine several measuring tapes laid through the same room.

The metric now gives us real assembly rules:

- If two drawings describe the same tape, their markings must transform consistently.
- If two tapes touch the same observer, they share that observer's clock direction.
- If one tape hands a measurement to the next, the middle calibration must truly match or an
  explicit adapter remains between them.
- If many tape directions coexist in four-dimensional spacetime, their mutual lengths and angles
  cannot be arbitrary. They must all fit into one four-dimensional Lorentzian Gram matrix.

Those are meaningful constraints. They tell us whether the instrument assembly is geometrically
possible.

They still do not choose the music. Our first three examples were complete on paper, but Charles
correctly noticed that several instruments were held still. We withdrew the phrase “fully live”
and repeated the test more strictly.

In the corrected examples, every metric and pair block changes, every mixing component changes,
and every block makes a separately nonzero contribution to the final pair metric. Even then we can
construct three exact assemblies obeying the same overlap rules:

1. one stays equally loud through every reciprocal regime;
2. one changes monotonically;
3. one becomes loud toward both ends and quiet in the middle.

The third pattern is therefore real and native to the metric's reciprocal response. It survived the
no-shortcut treatment with the angular and mixing instruments inside the pair relation. But simply
turning on every instrument still does not force nature to play that pattern, because active
instruments can cancel one another coherently.

In orchestra language: we have derived more of the rules saying which instruments can share a stage
and remain synchronized. We have also confirmed that the desired loud-quiet-loud chord is available.
We have not yet found the conductor or score that decides which coordinated history is physical.
That future rule could reject the constructed cancellations, but their rejection does not follow
merely from insisting that no instrument be silent.
