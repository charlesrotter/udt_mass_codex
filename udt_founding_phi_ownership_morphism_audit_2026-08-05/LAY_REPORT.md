# Lay report

The foundation gives UDT a very clear **grammar** for comparing clocks and rulers. If you hand it a
depth between two observers, it tells you exactly how the clock side and ruler side change, how the
comparison reverses, and how several comparisons combine.

What it does not give is the **address book** saying what depth belongs to every actual pair of
observers in a complete universe.

We had also been asking the address-book question in a slightly misleading way: “Which value of
`phi` belongs to every point?” The exact geometry says that a pointwise `phi` can shift when the
reference ruler and clock convention shifts, while the complete physical geometry stays identical.
It is like changing the zero on a height map. The individual altitude number changes; the complete
terrain and properly compared heights do not.

That does not make `phi` meaningless. It means the founding physical idea is relational: the depth
between observers is primary, and a field `phi(x)` is a convenient potential when a branch allows
all those comparisons to be written as differences.

There is already one clean example. In a stationary geometry with a natural time-flow direction,
the metric itself compares the clock norms at two locations. That produces an exact relative depth
and is unaffected by how the common time direction is normalized. But a general changing geometry
may not have that stationary landmark.

So the missing piece is smaller and better aimed than before:

> Find the UDT-native two-observer depth law. It must reduce to the stationary clock-norm ratio,
> survive changes of reference description, distinguish signed comparison from ordinary
> nonnegative distance, and work honestly across paths, angular structure, and global completion.

This audit does not invent that law. It shows why demanding a universal absolute `phi` was likely
the wrong target and why more local curvature algebra could not find it.
