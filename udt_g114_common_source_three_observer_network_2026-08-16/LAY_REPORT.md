# G114 lay report

The simplification held up.

Think of each observer as sending a complete musical phrase to the same microphone. The metric
determines how each phrase travels. At the microphone, however, three recordings cannot be spliced
together merely because they have the same number of audio channels. Their channel orientations
and timing calibration must be identified at the junction.

Once that junction is typed correctly, the algebra is simple:

- each ray carries a complete position-and-direction state;
- those complete states compose exactly;
- a caustic can squash the position picture to zero without destroying the full state;
- going around three observers measures the rotation accumulated at the common-source junction.

The surprising part is that complete algebraic closure is not enough. Each point observer uses
only a two-dimensional slice of the four-dimensional propagation state. Those slices may align at
the source, intersect in only one direction, or miss each other completely. The metric history and
the stated source query determine that alignment.

This gives us a native, falsifiable compatibility test rather than an invented selection
mechanism. But we still must say what source pattern or source boundary is being compared. The
metric can propagate a supplied source pattern; a single source point does not contain an entire
image, polarization field, or brightness law.

In orchestra language: we now know how the instruments arrive at the same stage and how to test
whether they are actually playing the same chord. We have not yet derived what sheet music the
source places on their stands.
